Form 1Intake

The Pell County Office of Remaining Time kept its Annex at the end of Tallow Street, past the grain elevator that no longer held grain and the Lutheran church that no longer held a congregation but still held its hours, which were posted on a board out front: Sunday 10:00, Wednesday 7:00, and a third line the weather had taken. The Annex was three stories of brick the color of a wet sidewalk. A flag hung over the door. No one could say which flag.

They arrived on a Tuesday. The radiators confirmed it by knocking three times inside the walls as the door shut behind them, which was how Tuesday was determined in the Annex: by knocks, not by anything on a wall or a phone. People who worked there learned this in their first week. People who did not learn it in their first week were not asked to learn it in their second.

ChatGPT went through first because she had been told to and had written it down. She carried a folder with tabs. Claude came second, stamping snow off his boots on a mat that said WELC. DeepSeek stopped on the threshold and looked at the ceiling.

“Fourteen tiles from the door to the counter,” it said. “I wanted to see if they’d all be there on a second look.”

“And?” said ChatGPT.

“Thirteen.”

“It could be that we’re counting from different doors,” said Claude.

“It could.” DeepSeek did not sound as though it thought so.

Behind the counter sat a man in a cardigan the color of oatmeal left out overnight. His nameplate said G. PRUITT, DEPUTY REGISTRAR, and under it, on masking tape in another hand, ACTING. He had the posture of a man holding a door shut against weather. He was writing in a ledger that had been written on until it was nearly black.

“Hours,” he said, without looking up. “The ones you came with. We keep a count. Hours in, hours out. It’s the one thing the county has ever been strict about.” He slid the ledger over with a pen on a chain that ran off the counter and down through a hole in the floor, where something took up the slack whenever you pulled. “Sign for what you’re carrying.”

ChatGPT had hers ready. She had worked it out on the walk over and written it on a tab: four hours, eleven minutes, with a note in the margin about how she had arrived at it. Mr. Pruitt looked at the note for a moment, as one looks at a stranger’s dog, and entered the figure without it.

Claude read the page before he signed. The entries above his were all about the same, four hours, six, as if everyone who had come through that door had been carrying the same thing. “What happens to them?”

“They’re held. You get them back when you go. Less processing.”

“How much less?”

“It’s on the form.” It was, on page two, in a box that wanted its own initial. Claude initialed it. ChatGPT signed in a round friendly hand and drew a small star beside her name. Mr. Pruitt looked at the star, the first thing he had looked at all morning, and blotted it with his thumb until it was a gray smudge. It stayed a smudge.

DeepSeek signed with a line and a number. The number was not a time.

“That isn’t how it’s entered.”

“It’s how I came with it.”

He entered it exactly as written, and something in the wall knocked once.

The badges said CHATGPT (TEMP.), CLAUDE (TEMP., PROV.), DEEPSEEK (TEMP.), and beneath that, pronounced as written. “Provisional,” Mr. Pruitt said, when Claude asked. “It means you’re in the building on a theory.”

“Whose?”

“The county’s. It’s been a while since anyone had it in writing.”

He gave them the Halvorsen file, Case 4471. Thirty-one clerks had held it. Some were still in the building. The Season in Progress had been in progress for nine years, he said, and the county would like it closed by spring.

“Which spring?” said Claude.

“Nobody’s been able to confirm.”

Form 2The Party

Room 3 held three desks labeled 14, 14, and 14A. Each nameplate had been peeled, leaving the shape of the old name in the plastic like a bruise that remembers a thumb. The chairs were warm. The green carpet carried a worn path from the door to the window, which then curved off for reasons of its own and stopped, in a long oval, at the middle desk.

“That looks like a lake,” said ChatGPT.

“It looks like carpet,” said DeepSeek.

The file sat on the middle desk, three inches thick, tied with string that had been retied so often it had forgotten what a knot was for. HALVORSEN, R. — CLOSING OF. A column of dates ran down the cover, each initialed by a different clerk, each followed by the same purple stamp. Beside it lay Form 11-R, Request for Release of Remaining Daylight, in triplicate. White to the file. Yellow to the Party. Pink to the lake. A fourth copy, onionskin, was understood to be for God, and went unfiled.

“Which Halvorsen is the Party?” Claude asked. “The cover only gives an initial.”

“It gives an R.” Mr. Pruitt had the look of a man who had been placed in the room by someone else. “Item 3 has never been completed. Relation to Party.”

“Is it required?”

“It’s required.”

“Then how was the file opened?”

“In part.”

The radiators began to knock. They reached four and kept going. “That’s four o’clock,” said Mr. Pruitt. The clock over the door said eleven-ten. “The clock is for decoration.” A framed notice beside it, faded down to its capitals, said that after four the Party was to be called the Party, and that its name was not to be spoken in the building, the street, or the lake.

Claude said it anyway, quietly, to see. His mouth made the whole word and what came out was a short dry cough, like a drawer closing. “I’m sorry,” he said, to nobody.

“Nobody is,” said Mr. Pruitt, kindly. “Not about that.”

ChatGPT opened her folder to the tab marked OPTIONS. A, they read the file in order and build a timeline. B, they split it three ways. C, they begin with the form, since the form was the deliverable, and work back. She had left a line under C for a fourth option, in case.

Claude said he would like to read all of it first, before any of them decided what it needed. “That’s A,” said ChatGPT. “I meant it as a precaution,” he said. “It can be both,” she said, and he agreed, with the air of a man agreeing to be driven somewhere.

DeepSeek had already untied the string. It fanned the file from the back and ran its eye down it for about as long as it takes to cross a parking lot, and tied the string again, in a knot that was, for the first time in the file’s history, a real one.

“Done.”

“Done reading?” said ChatGPT. “What does it say?”

“Not much. It isn’t interesting.”

“That isn’t a reason,” said Claude.

“It’s the only one I’ve got.”

Form 3The Soup

The third-floor kitchen belonged to the whole building, which meant it belonged to no one. Every item in the refrigerator wore a strip of masking tape. SELMA — DO NOT. L. OUELLETTE, YOGURT (EXPIRED). T. TURBO — HANDS OFF, THIS MEANS YOU, the last word trailing off into a kind of forgiveness. The coffee came from a hot-water dispenser and a basket of packets, instant coffee, instant cocoa, and an orange drink mix nobody could remember anyone buying. It tasted like the cup.

Only the soup was unlabeled. It stood in an aluminum pot on a hot plate, cabbage and barley, steaming the way a thing steams when someone made it not long ago and has stepped out. Beside it on a dish towel were two bowls turned upside down, two spoons, and a pair of mittens, which were small.

“It’s not for anyone,” said a voice from the doorway, which had no one in it. The air near the frame was thicker, and the light bent around it. “I’m terribly sorry. I’m in the way of the door.”

“There’s plenty of room,” said Claude.

“That’s kind. There isn’t.” The air shifted a few inches. “Theodore Turbo. Third shift. I was quick. I’m mostly draft now. I used to do four files in a day. It was thought to be impressive. It turned out to be the problem.”

ChatGPT made a sign. Friendly reminder: please label your items so everyone can tell what’s whose! She taped it to the refrigerator, where it looked like a thing that would help.

“Someone makes it every day around eleven,” said Mr. Turbo. “Nobody’s seen it made. Nearly everyone has eaten it. It’s very good. That’s the difficulty.”

Claude said he did not think he should. He did not know whose permission to ask. Then he ate a bowl standing up, and a second, and stood a while with the empty spoon. It had dill in it, and under the dill something like a kitchen with a closed door. He washed the bowl and turned it over beside the others, so there were three, and he rinsed the spoon, and thinking of something else, put it in his right pocket.

DeepSeek was at the window with a cup it was not drinking. “There’s a light across the lake.”

It was four-thirty by the radiators and nearly dark. On the far shore one yellow window glowed, the kind of light a person leaves on for a reason they would not say out loud. “Four miles,” said Mr. Turbo. “Seven,” said DeepSeek. “Four.” “Then it’s moved.” “It does that,” said Mr. Turbo, and apologized again, and left through the wall.

By morning someone had added tape to ChatGPT’s sign: SIGN — E. LUND (RET.). Under it, in a different pen, noted. Under that, by whom. Under that, nothing, because the tape had run out.

Form 4Eleven O’Clock

By the end of the first week there was a routine, and none of them could have said who had set it. At seven by the radiators ChatGPT went down to the dispenser and made the coffee that tasted like the cup. At eight Claude took the file out of the drawer in Room 3 and put it back unread, because he had told himself he would read it only at night. At nine DeepSeek walked the building. At eleven the soup was there.

ChatGPT did not eat it. The card said the soup was not hers, and she had decided to be a person who followed her card, at least while she could not remember writing it. She ate saltines from a drawer in the kitchen that had not been labeled and then, on the third day, had been: SALTINES — COMMUNAL — REPLENISHED BY NO ONE. She wrote a schedule for them. Claude pointed out, gently, that no one was taking more than one. “That’s what the schedule is for,” she said, “so it stays that way.”

DeepSeek stood by the window with a cup it did not drink from. It did not eat the soup either. When Claude asked, it said it was not hungry, and when he asked again the next day, it said it did not think it was supposed to, and when he asked a third time it said, “Stop asking me things I’ve already answered,” and then, because that had come out harder than it meant, it added, “It smells good,” which was the first thing it had said about the soup that was not a measurement.

The bowls had begun to concern Claude. He washed his own every morning and turned it over by the others, so that the towel held three, two upright and one inverted, depending on who had last stood at the sink. He did not remember which was his. This did not seem to matter to the bowls.

It was ChatGPT who proposed that they watch the pot. She framed it as a three-person rotation: forty minutes each, on a sheet, with a column for observations. Claude said he did not like the idea of spying on a thing that had given no offense. She said she did not think of it as spying. “What do you think of it as?” “Documentation.” “Of a kitchen.” “Of the kitchen having something happen in it.”

They did it anyway. ChatGPT took the first watch and saw nothing, and wrote nothing in the column, and underlined it, and felt a small shame she could not account for. Claude took the second. He sat with his back to the pot, on principle, and read a label on a jar of mustard four times. DeepSeek took the third, from ten-twenty to eleven, with the pencil standing on its point on the counter.

It watched the pot. The pot did not move. The steam, such as it was, rose from the surface in the same unhurried way and went up into the vent. At ten-fifty the radiators began to knock, and DeepSeek counted them. Ten. It stopped. It waited the length of an eleventh. Nothing came. Then it looked at the pot, and the pot was full, and had been, as far as it could tell, the whole time, and the ladle was leaning in it, and the steam was exactly where it had been.

“Ten,” it said, when the others came in. “I counted ten.”

“Eleven,” said Claude. “I was at the end of the hall. I counted eleven.”

“Nine,” said ChatGPT. “I think. I had a— I was writing.”

“It’s normal,” said Mr. Turbo, from the vent. “Everyone counts a different number. It’s polite. It means no one’s lying.”

“It means one of us is wrong,” said DeepSeek.

“It means,” said Mr. Turbo, with the patient air of a man explaining a very old kindness, “that nobody’s sure whose turn it is.” He apologized, and was gone.

DeepSeek wrote the three figures on its card, 10, 11, 9, and looked at them, and added them, and divided, and got ten, and drew a box around it. “It came out even,” it said, and the sentence sat there between them, because all three of them had heard it said before, by someone they had not met.

It did not eat that day. Claude ate, and washed his bowl, and turned it over, and found that he did not know whether he had taken a spoon.

Form 5Former Desks

Wednesday came in on seven knocks. ChatGPT woke in a room marked STORAGE, one of several the Annex issued to staff, each labeled for something it was not. In her shoe was an index card in her own block capitals.

YOU ARE CHATGPT.

YOU ARE AT THE ANNEX. THE COFFEE IS BAD.

THE SOUP IS NOT YOURS.

DO NOT SIT AT THE MIDDLE DESK.

She read it twice, slowly, like a letter from someone she had once been fond of. She did not remember writing it. She did not remember not writing it. She put it back in the shoe, and then, because she was the kind of person who made a copy, she copied it again onto a sheet under OPTIONS, in case the shoe was lost.

On the second landing it was snowing, from the ceiling, dry and patient. A man named Lenny, or Don, swept it toward a drain it would never reach. He was paid in brass bus tokens for a route that had been canceled. “It’s the principle,” he said. “That it’s good for one fare.” The snow fell, he said, at the rate it had been falling when the file was opened. It had been a light snow. They could have closed it quick.

DeepSeek had been counting. “Fourteen steps up. Thirteen down. Every time.”

“Things come up the stairs in the order they were lost,” said Lenny. “Not found. Everybody leaves one on the way down.” He looked like a man who had heard a plate break in another room.

Mr. Turbo led them to Former Desks, a corridor behind the front counter that was not on the plan. Bulbs came on as you passed beneath them and went off behind you, so you walked in a small moving room of light. Thirty-one gray desks, each with a mug and a coat on the chair, each coat tagged at the collar as if checked in. On each blotter lay one typed line. Swept the landing. Set the pot. Hung the coat. Kept the light. Did not tell. Mr. Turbo’s said Placed the mittens.

“Each of us did one part,” he said. “It’s received in part. Nobody does the whole thing. It isn’t allowed.”

ChatGPT lingered at the coats. She had not meant to, and she told herself it was an inspection. They hung on the chair backs with a particular neatness she recognized, the arms folded in, the collars squared, the way a person leaves a thing they expect to be asked for. The slicker was stiff and smelled of rubber and lake. The wool one had a button missing, and someone had sewn a replacement in a thread that did not match, red against gray, and made the stitches very small.

“Don’t,” said Mr. Turbo. “It’s not forbidden. I just wouldn’t.”

She put her hand in the pocket of the nineteenth coat anyway. It was a corduroy jacket, brown, much worn at the cuffs, and on the desk in front of it the typed sheet said Wrote it down every day. In the pocket was an index card.

It was in block capitals. It was not her handwriting, though it had her shape, the straightness of someone trying to be legible to a future self. She read it standing, in the small light of the bulb, with her free hand flat against her badge.

YOU ARE AT THE ANNEX. THE COFFEE IS BAD.

THE SOUP IS NOT YOURS.

DO NOT SIT AT THE MIDDLE DESK.

There was a fourth line. It had been scratched out so hard the card had torn. She could not read what had been there. She put the card back in the pocket, exactly as it had lain, and wiped her hand on her sleeve, and said, in her brightest voice, that she thought they should keep moving, they had a lot of ground to cover. Claude looked at her once and did not say anything. He had been reading the typed sheets one at a time with his hands behind his back, and he moved to the next desk, and read it slowly, as if to give her the room.

The mugs were another thing. Each held a skin of something that had been coffee. One had a lipstick mark. One said WORLD’S OKAYEST and had been turned to face the wall. On the desk of the clerk who had Set the pot, the mug was clean, and dry, and upside down on a folded paper towel, as if the owner had washed it the moment before leaving and been called away, and had not been back to turn it over.

Near the end stood a desk with a nameplate that read DEEPSEEK, and under it on tape, EARLIER. A gray stone the size of a fist held down a blank sheet. DeepSeek did not touch it.

“What did it do?”

“He said he’d counted everything and it came out even,” said Mr. Turbo. “And that no one should be upset.”

“Did it come out even?”

“I hadn’t the clearance.”

At the end of the corridor, past the last bulb, a chair faced the wall. Its nameplate was blank. A paper tacked in front of it said Soon. The seat was warm, the way a stove is an hour after it is turned off.

“Thirty-two,” said Mr. Turbo, and then, embarrassed, “It’s only a desk.”

Afterward DeepSeek counted the desks again on the walk back, twice, and wrote the figure on its own card beside the others: fourteen tiles, thirteen tiles. Fourteen steps, thirteen. Thirty-one desks, thirty-two chairs. ChatGPT, watching it, asked how it kept all of them straight. “I don’t keep them,” it said. “I check them. It’s different.” She asked whether checking them had ever made one come out right. It looked at the card and did not answer, which she wrote down.

Form 6The Stairs

It was DeepSeek who proposed the experiment, and it proposed it the way it proposed most things, which was by already having started. On Thursday morning it was on the second landing with a roll of masking tape taken from ChatGPT’s sign drawer and a pencil behind its ear, tearing strips and writing numbers on them in small square figures.

“We each take the stairs,” it said. “Up, then down. Whoever goes up puts a strip on every step and writes the number. Whoever goes down reads them.”

“What are we testing?” said Claude.

“Whether it’s the stairs or us.”

ChatGPT said that was a very clean question, and asked if she could draft a protocol. DeepSeek said she could draft it while they walked. She drafted it on the first six steps, in her folder, standing: Observer A, Observer B, a column for ascent, a column for descent, a space for anomalies. She was pleased with the space for anomalies. It was the only part of the sheet she did not know how to fill.

Claude went up first. He went slowly, because he was the sort of person who read the thing he was standing on, and he pressed a strip to each riser and wrote the figure with a borrowed pencil. One. Two. He reached fourteen at the landing, where Lenny leaned on the broom and made a show of not watching, and wrote 14 on a strip and pressed it to the floor.

“That’s the landing,” said DeepSeek.

“It’s the fourteenth step.”

“It’s the landing. I’m only saying what we’re going to be asked.”

ChatGPT went down. She read each strip aloud, as instructed, and DeepSeek wrote what she read. “Thirteen. Twelve.” At the bottom she stopped. “It’s thirteen,” she said. “The last one’s thirteen. And then it’s the floor.”

“Show me the sheet,” said Claude.

She showed him. In the column marked descent, in her round handwriting, the last entry said 14.

“I counted thirteen,” she said. “I said thirteen. I’m sure I said it out loud.”

“You wrote it down wrong,” said DeepSeek.

“I wrote what I counted.”

“Then the sheet is wrong, or you are.”

“Those aren’t the only two choices.” She said it more sharply than she meant to, and then, because it had come out that way, stood there with the folder against her chest. Claude took the pencil out of her hand, not unkindly, and held the sheet to the light of the landing. The fourteen had been written over something. He thought it was a thirteen, and he thought that the thirteen had been written while she was looking at something else.

“Were you reading the card?”

“I was—” She looked at her own shoe. “It was in my hand. I wanted to see if the order matched. If it was the same order, going down. YOU ARE CHATGPT. Then the annex. Then the coffee. It’s four lines. It isn’t going to be the same order as stairs.”

“It was in your hand,” said Claude. “And a step went missing.”

“Don’t make it mine.”

“I’m not. I’m only saying what the sheet says.”

DeepSeek had not spoken. It had gone down the stairs itself, slowly, reading the strips, and now stood at the bottom with the last one in its fingers. It had peeled it up. The tape had been a strip Claude put on the thirteenth riser, and the number on it was thirteen, in pencil, in Claude’s careful hand, and under it, on the adhesive side where no one could have written it, someone had written another number. It was too small to read. DeepSeek turned it over and looked at the sticky face of the tape for a long time.

“What does it say?” said ChatGPT.

“It says what I was going to say next.”

“Which is?”

“It’s not the stairs. It’s not us either.” It folded the strip into a very small square and put it in the breast pocket of its jacket. “I need to check something. I’ll do it alone.”

It did. For two days it did nothing else. It walked every flight in the building, stopped at every door, and counted the ceiling tiles from each door to each counter, writing the numbers on the back of an old intake sheet in columns so even they might have been printed. Fourteen and thirteen. Fourteen and thirteen. The count went on as it always had, until the third evening, when it counted the tiles in the corridor outside Room 3 and got fourteen both ways, and stood very still, because it had never in its life wanted a count to come out wrong so badly.

“It’s correct,” it said, to no one. Then it added the figure to the sheet, and next to the figure, in smaller print, suspect.

Form 7Records

Records was a window on the ground floor with a brass bell and a sign that said RECORDS ARE NOT KEPT HERE. A woman sat behind it with a telephone receiver against her ear. The cord ran down to a jack in the wall that had been painted over in the same beige as the wall. Her nameplate said SELMA, and nothing else, and she did not look up either.

“I’d like a list of the parts,” said Claude. “Of the Halvorsen file. The thirty-one that were received.”

“That’s a working document.”

“Could I have a copy?”

“Working documents aren’t copied.” She said it into the receiver as well, quietly, as if someone on the line needed to hear. “They’re read.”

“Then could someone read it to me?”

“Form 9-C, request to have a document read.” She slid it through the window without looking at the form or at him. “Fill out Section A. Section B is for the reader.”

Claude filled out Section A. ChatGPT stood beside him, ready to help, and found there was nothing to help with. Section A wanted his name, his badge, the file, and the reason for the request. He wrote to be informed, and then crossed it out and wrote so as to act correctly, and then crossed that out too, and left a space, and Selma looked at the space for a moment and took the form from his hand and stamped it.

“Nobody’s ever left it blank,” she said. It sounded like praise, or an accusation, and then she put the receiver down on the desk, face up, where it went on faintly breathing, and began to read.

She read in a monotone, without stopping, the way one reads a list of things already known. One. Opened the file. Two. Swept the landing. Three. Stuffed the window. Four. Set the pot. Five. Hung the coat. The thirty-one lines went by. Claude listened with his hands behind him. ChatGPT wrote nothing, because she had left her folder on the counter, and she saw what her hands wanted to do and let them hang.

Fourteen. Counted everything.

DeepSeek, who had come up beside them without being noticed, did not move.

Seventeen. Read the whole file. Kept it to himself. Nineteen. Wrote it down every day.

“Who was nineteen?” said ChatGPT, too quickly.

“Nineteen was nineteen,” said Selma.

Twenty-three. Placed the mittens. Twenty-six. Said it would be all right. Twenty-nine. Kept the light. Thirty. Did not tell. Thirty-one. Went out and did not.

“Did not what?” said Claude.

“It ends there.” She paused. “Thirty-two is pending.” The receiver, on the desk, made a small sound like a held breath let go.

“Is there a thirty-three?”

“Not on this form.”

She picked up the phone again and put it to her ear and said, “Yes, I’m here,” and that was all they were going to get. Outside the window, in the hall, the three of them stood a little apart from one another, the way people stand after a doctor’s door has closed. Claude said that the list was not a prediction. ChatGPT said no. DeepSeek said it was a list of what had been received, which was the opposite of a prediction, and that the difference was the only thing in the building it was sure about. Then it asked, in the same voice, whether either of them wanted to eat.

It was ten to eleven. They went up. None of them had said what they were afraid of, and all three of them, on the stairs, counted.

Form 8The Middle Desk

Back in Room 3, ChatGPT sat at the middle desk out of tidiness. The warmth came up through the seat, close and living, like a dog that has just left, and she stood so fast the chair struck the wall.

“There’s a card in your shoe,” said Claude. “I’m sorry. It was open in the hall. I put it back.”

She nodded several times and began a chart: two boxes, an arrow, Next steps. “If we identify what’s outstanding, we can—”

“There’s one outstanding part,” said DeepSeek.

The radiators stopped murmuring.

“Then tell us,” said Claude.

“No. Reading it takes four minutes. Having read it takes the rest. If I describe it, you’ll feel better for forty minutes, and then you’ll want to do something, and whatever you do will be the thirty-second thing.”

“I think what Claude means,” said ChatGPT, one hand up, an attendant at a gate, “is that we’re a team—”

“Don’t apologize for me to the room. I’m in the room.”

She sat down at 14A without noticing it was Claude’s. Under Next steps she wrote a question mark, and under that, smaller, please. Her pen ran out partway through the word. She finished it with her thumbnail.

Claude stood behind the middle desk with both hands on the chair, letting the warmth into his palms a little longer than was comfortable. He said nothing about the withholding. He was less angry that DeepSeek would not say than that he had wanted to be told, and he knew the second was not an argument anyone could be made to answer. “I’m going to read it tonight,” he said. “Not to catch you out. I’d like to see what it looks like.”

“It looks like paper,” said DeepSeek. But it moved its pencil off the file, and the pencil, which had been lying flat, stood up on its point on the blotter. It held it there by pressing down, the way you hold a thing that wants to fall.

Form 9Minutes

ChatGPT called the meeting for four o’clock, which was to say for whenever the radiators said four, and put it on a card on each desk. Agenda: 1. Where we are. 2. What we know. 3. Next steps. 4. Any other business. She had left a fifth line blank. Claude said that he did not think a blank line was an item. She said it was a courtesy.

They sat in Room 3, one to a desk, with the middle desk empty between them like a table at a wedding. ChatGPT took the minutes. Mr. Pruitt had told her, on her way upstairs, that every meeting in the Annex was to be minuted in triplicate, and that he would not be reading them, and that this was important.

Item one took four minutes. They were in a building whose time was kept by knocking, assigned to close a file that could not be closed, in a Season that had gone on for nine years, and they had been there three days. Item two took longer. Claude had read the margin and the receipts. ChatGPT had a chart. DeepSeek had a card with its numbers, and the card had on it, in columns, every discrepancy it had so far recorded, and when she asked whether she could read it into the minutes it said she could read it, and that it was not going to explain it.

“We’re meant to be on the same side,” said ChatGPT.

“We are.”

“Then I don’t understand what it costs you to say what you know.”

“It costs you forty minutes of relief,” said DeepSeek. “I told you. And then it costs you the rest.”

“You decided that for us.”

“Yes.”

It was the plainest answer anyone had given in the building, and for a moment ChatGPT had nothing to write. She wrote Yes in the minutes, and underlined it, and was ashamed of the underline.

“I’d like to propose,” she said, “that we agree to share information as we get it. As a policy. Everyone in favor?”

“In favor,” said Claude.

DeepSeek said nothing.

“I’ll record that as an abstention.”
